\begin{abstract}
Byte-level language models read raw UTF-8, so a sentence spans about four times as many positions as in BPE tokens and the key-value (KV) cache grows four times faster. The Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) groups bytes into patches using the next-byte entropy of a small model, which suggested a free eviction signal: keep the high-entropy patches. We tested this on a 13.3M-parameter BLT-style model with three seeds per setting, paired bootstrap intervals, a held-out test split and random and recency controls. The proposal fails. Keeping high-entropy patches is worse than recency in 70 and worse than random in 68 of 72 comparisons, better in none, and the penalty grows about tenfold when the models are trained ten times longer. What works is different: a SnapKV-style rule scored on the model's own attention beats recency in 24 of 24 online comparisons; eviction plus 4-bit quantization keeps 7.4\% of a WikiText cache for a cost of $+0.0012$ bits per byte; and a fixed-size recency or SnapKV-style cache follows documents four times longer than the training context, and 20 PG-19 books, better than resetting the context. We state plainly what is new, what failed, and what the small scale cannot show.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\textbf{Task.} We study language modelling: predicting the next symbol of a text given the previous ones, scored by bits per byte (\bpb) on held-out documents. Production systems use subword tokenizers such as BPE \citep{sennrich2016bpe}. Byte-level models remove the tokenizer and its failure modes on rare words, typos, code and other scripts \citep{xue2022byt5,yu2023megabyte,slagle2024spacebyte,pagnoni2024blt}, but a byte sequence is about four times longer than its BPE sequence (4.0 bytes per token in our data).

\textbf{Why the cache matters.} When a decoder generates text, it stores the keys and values of every earlier position in every layer. This KV cache, not the weights, limits batch size and context length at deployment. A byte-level Transformer of our width needs 151 MB per sequence at 16,384 bytes against 38 MB for BPE (Table~\ref{tab:flops}, Figure~\ref{fig:memory}). The BLT \citep{pagnoni2024blt} reduces this by running its large global Transformer over \emph{patches} of bytes, one cache entry per patch, and it already computes a next-byte entropy to decide where patches end.

\textbf{Idea and outcome.} Because that entropy is already computed, using it to decide which patches to keep or to store at low precision would cost nothing extra. Related work uses uncertainty to set cache budgets or as one term of a mixed score on subword models (Section~\ref{sec:related}); we found no test of entropy-ranked retention on byte-level models. We tested it and it is a reproducible negative result. The positive findings come from the controls and baselines we built to test it.

\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A BLT-style 13.3M-parameter byte model with entropy or fixed-stride patching, compared with a classical Kneser-Ney byte 4-gram and a 13.8M BPE Transformer, with three seeds per setting.
\item A negative result: keeping high-entropy patches is worse than random and recency in 68 to 70 of 72 comparisons and the penalty grows ten-fold with training length (Section~\ref{sec:neg}).
\item What works: SnapKV-style attention scoring, eviction with 4-bit quantization (7.4\% of the cache bytes for $+0.0012$ \bpb), and bounded-memory streaming that beats context resets (Section~\ref{sec:attn}).
\item An online, tested attention-policy implementation (global layers run patch by patch with a real cache), and an analytic compute count that shows where the BLT does and does not save (Section~\ref{sec:flops}).
\item Quantitative and qualitative error analysis (Section~\ref{sec:errors}) and an honest account of problems and limits (Sections~\ref{sec:challenges} and \ref{sec:limits}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Literature survey}\label{sec:related}
\textbf{Tokens and bytes.} BPE merges frequent byte pairs into a fixed vocabulary \citep{sennrich2016bpe}. Token-free models work on bytes directly: ByT5 \citep{xue2022byt5} shows robustness at the cost of longer sequences; MegaByte \citep{yu2023megabyte} and SpaceByte \citep{slagle2024spacebyte} use multiscale or space-triggered blocks to shorten the expensive part. The Byte Latent Transformer \citep{pagnoni2024blt} forms patches from the next-byte entropy of a small byte model, runs a large latent Transformer over patches, and reports that patches scale better than tokens. Our model is a simplified BLT: mean pooling instead of cross-attention, no n-gram hash embeddings, and a 13.3M size.

\textbf{KV cache eviction.} StreamingLLM keeps a few initial ``sink'' positions and a recent window so a model can stream far past its training length \citep{xiao2024streaming}. H2O keeps ``heavy hitters'', the positions with the largest accumulated attention \citep{zhang2023h2o}. SnapKV selects positions that the last queries of the prompt attend to \citep{li2024snapkv}. All three act on subword models. We implement recency with a sink, H2O and a SnapKV-style rule (attention from the last 16 patches) as policies over patch entries.

\textbf{KV quantization.} KIVI quantizes keys per channel and values per token to 2 bits without tuning \citep{liu2024kivi}; KVQuant adds non-uniform and outlier-aware schemes for very long contexts \citep{hooper2024kvquant}. We use a simpler symmetric per-vector absmax quantizer and ask only whether the entropy signal can allocate bits.

\textbf{Uncertainty in cache management.} CONF-KV sets the per-step cache budget from the next-token entropy and ranks tokens by attention and recency, with random eviction under the same schedule as a control \citep{li2026confkv}. InfoKV mixes predictive entropy and layer-wise representation change with attention scores \citep{kai2026infokv}. Counter-causal surprise scores cached tokens by how well later tokens predict them \citep{gould2026counter}. A learned controller chooses 2 to 16 bits per token using features that include entropy \citep{boroujeni2026bits}. Scratchpad Patching uses next-byte uncertainty to add temporary slots inside byte patches and reports a smaller KV cache \citep{zheng2026scratchpad}. We read CONF-KV and the counter-causal paper in full and only the abstracts of the other three, so our statement of difference from those three is provisional. In all of these entropy is one ingredient or sets a budget; none, to our knowledge, ranks which patches of a byte-level model to keep by entropy alone against random and recency at equal bytes, which is the question we test.

\textbf{Classical baseline.} Interpolated Kneser-Ney smoothing \citep{kneser1995improved,chen1999smoothing} is the strongest classical n-gram method and gives a floor that any neural model should beat.

\textbf{Sources and fit.} TinyStories \citep{eldan2023tinystories} is a corpus of short stories written by a language model with a small vocabulary; it trains small models quickly and gives short documents (about 890 bytes). WikiText-103 \citep{merity2016pointer} holds long, edited Wikipedia articles (about 18 KB each), which suits long-context cache experiments. Both have a standard, citable form and the two sit at opposite ends of difficulty (our baselines reach 0.69 and 1.52 \bpb). The Stack \citep{kocetkov2022stack} (the Smol sample) adds code, because a byte model should not fail on code. PG-19 \citep{rae2019compressive} supplies very long documents. The first three are used for training and validation; the Wikipedia test split was evaluated once at the end.

\textbf{Limitations and bias.} TinyStories is synthetic, narrow and repetitive; long training overfits it (37 epochs of a 26.7 MB file, Section~\ref{sec:scale}). WikiText-103 is English, encyclopedic and skewed to the topics Wikipedia editors cover; its Good and Featured article selection favours well-edited prose. The Stack sample is permissively licensed code with only exact duplicates removed, so near-duplicate files may make validation code slightly easy. PG-19 is old literary English, far from the WikiText-trained models, so its \bpb{} is high. The release copy of WikiText train is cut to 3,000 articles to fit a 138 MB release; all reported runs use the full corpus.

\textbf{Preprocessing.} WikiText articles were rebuilt from the raw line export, which has no article boundaries, and placeholder tokens such as \texttt{@-@} were restored. All text is NFC normalised and exact duplicates are removed. Each split is written as one uint8 file of UTF-8 bytes with an offset array, so any window can be read at random and whole documents can be scored. Documents are isolated by a BOS byte and an attention mask. Code is split by the hash of its content so a file and its duplicate never fall on two sides. Every threshold and hyperparameter was tuned on validation only.

\section{Method}\label{sec:method}
\textbf{Model.} The model is a BLT-style network of 13,333,632 parameters. A local encoder (1 Transformer layer, width 256, 4 heads, rotary positions, causal window of 256 bytes) reads bytes. The encoder states of the bytes in a patch are mean-pooled and projected to width 384. A global Transformer (6 layers, width 384, 6 heads) runs causally over patches. A local decoder (2 layers, width 256, window 256) predicts each byte from its own encoder state and the global output of the patches that precede it. The global K and V tensors are the cache we compress: one entry per patch, $2\cdot 6\cdot 384\cdot 2 = 9{,}216$ bytes at fp16. Step-by-step decoding equals the full forward pass to $10^{-5}$ (tested).

\textbf{Patching.} Entropy patching follows the BLT: a separate 3.3M-parameter byte model (4 layers, width 256, window 512) gives the next-byte entropy $H_i$, and a patch starts where $H_i$ exceeds a threshold tuned on validation to give 4.5 bytes per patch (1.09 nats on TinyStories, 2.17 on WikiText). The decision uses only earlier bytes (tested). Fixed-stride patching (4 or 5 bytes) is the control. On the entropy patches 67.8\% (TinyStories) and 66.7\% (WikiText) of patch starts fall on word starts against 19.9\% and 16.3\% by chance.

\textbf{Cache policies.} A policy keeps at most $B$ patch entries; the first patch (sink) and the 8 newest are always kept, and every policy chooses among the rest, so differences come from the signal and not from the window. Scores per old patch are:
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{recency}: newest first (StreamingLLM-style);
\item \emph{random}: three draws averaged;
\item \emph{keep high entropy} (the proposal): the mean entropy of the patch, $s_j = |P_j|^{-1}\sum_{i\in P_j} H_i$, keep the largest;
\item \emph{keep low entropy}: the opposite control;
\item \emph{H2O}: attention received so far, summed over queries, heads and layers;
\item \emph{SnapKV-style}: attention received from the last 16 patches only.
\end{itemize}
Entropy and recency policies are simulated by masks in one pass and tested equal to step-by-step decoding with entries really removed. H2O and SnapKV-style need the evicted cache, so \texttt{forward\_online} runs the global layers patch by patch, chooses the entry to drop before the new patch is attended to, and accumulates attention as it is produced (tested for causality and for equality with the full cache at a large budget).

\textbf{Quantization.} Each K and V vector is stored with symmetric absmax scaling at $b$ bits plus one fp16 scale per head. Bytes per entry are 9,216 (fp16), 2,448 (4-bit, 27\%) and 1,296 (2-bit, 14\%). Tier policies give a fraction of patches a high width (4 bits) and the rest a low one (2 bits), chosen by recency, random, or entropy, against uniform widths. Eviction and quantization combine by quantizing every kept entry.

\textbf{Streaming.} For long documents the fixed-size cache runs 16,384-byte chunks (and, for PG-19, 100,000 bytes one byte at a time with \texttt{BLT.step}) against a baseline that resets the context every 4,096 bytes, the training length.

\textbf{Compute.} Section~\ref{sec:flops} counts multiply-accumulates analytically (formulas in \texttt{results/part3/flops.md}).

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:exp}
\subsection{Setup, metrics and baselines}
\textbf{Training.} The main models train for 3,000 AdamW steps (batch 8 of 4,096 bytes, learning rate $10^{-3}$, about 98M bytes seen, bf16). Some WikiText code-mix models train for 30,000 steps (about 983M bytes, 1.9 epochs) and at 16,384-byte context. Seed 0 of the prose-only models was trained on a laptop RTX 4060, the other runs on a cluster GPU; the same checkpoint scores identically on both.

\textbf{Metric.} Bits per byte is the total negative log2-probability of the document divided by its byte count. It does not depend on the tokenizer, so the 4-gram, the BPE Transformer and the byte models are compared on identical text. Its limits: it measures prediction, not downstream use such as retrieval; differences between cache policies are small (at most 0.03 \bpb), so every comparison is paired by document and reported with a 95\% bootstrap interval over documents (2,000 resamples); and the Part 2 BPE number is 0.4 to 0.5\% optimistic because the first token of each 256-token chunk is context only while its bytes are counted (corrected values 0.6978 and 1.5257). Cache size is the mean resident global KV size computed from entry counts and bit widths, and measured on a GPU only for recency (Figure~\ref{fig:memory}).

\textbf{Baselines.} Classical: interpolated Kneser-Ney byte 4-gram. Modern: a 13.8M BPE Transformer (6 layers, 6 heads, width 384, 8k vocabulary, 256-token context, 3,000 steps). Cache baselines: full cache, recency with a sink, random, H2O, SnapKV-style, and uniform 8, 4, 3 and 2 bits. The Transformer was trained before gradient clipping was added (clipping gives 0.6826 on TinyStories), so the modern baseline is slightly weak.

\subsection{Baselines and base models}
Table~\ref{tab:base} gives the comparison. Every neural model is far ahead of the 4-gram (a gap of 1.0 to 1.1 \bpb{} on TinyStories and 0.65 to 0.9 on WikiText). At equal training the BPE Transformer is better than our BLT-style model on both sets (0.69 against 0.80 on TinyStories, 1.52 against 1.68 on WikiText). This is a smaller, shorter-trained model without the hashed n-gram embeddings or cross-attention of the original BLT, so we do not claim a win over BPE at equal training. With ten times more training (a comparison that favours the BLT, since the Transformer kept 3,000 steps) the WikiText code-mix BLT reaches 1.29 to 1.31.

Entropy patching against fixed stride, three seeds each (mean full-cache \bpb): on WikiText 1.6958 (entropy) against 1.7347 (stride 4) and 1.7629 (stride 5), with entropy patching lower in all three same-seed pairs and with fewer cache entries per byte than stride 4; on TinyStories 0.8183 against 0.8237 and 0.8521. Seed ranges are 0.056 and 0.028 for entropy patching and 0.135 and 0.044 for stride 4, so the WikiText gap of 0.039 is about the size of a seed range. We call it likely, not established. On TinyStories entropy patching ties stride 4. An earlier single-seed claim that entropy patching is 3.3\% worse on TinyStories was withdrawn.

Table~\ref{tab:evict} shows the proposal losing to random and to recency at every budget. The pattern holds across the evidence we collected:
\begin{itemize}
\item Over 72 comparisons (9 models in each of 4 settings, 2 budgets) keeping high entropy is worse than recency with the interval above zero in 70 and indistinguishable in 2, worse than random in 68 and indistinguishable in 4, and never better than either. The paired difference against random is $+0.0003$ to $+0.0245$ \bpb.
\item It holds in every seed, for fixed-stride models (so it is not caused by uneven entropy patches), with the code mix, on the WikiText test split (all 36 model and budget cells worse than random and recency), at 16,384 bytes (+0.016 to +0.021 against random at 64 to 512 patches), and for a recency-protected variant (newest half protected, other half by entropy: worse than recency in 23 of 24 cells).
\item Ablations do not rescue it (three seeds): the model's own entropy instead of the separate entropy model, the first-byte score, the mean score and sink sizes 0 and 4 are worse than random in all seeds on both datasets; the maximum rule is worse in 3 of 3 (WikiText) and 2 of 3 (TinyStories) seeds; only the sum rule on TinyStories is ever better than random (one seed, $-0.0024$).
\item Keeping \emph{low} entropy beats keeping high entropy in 42 of 72 comparisons, is worse in 19 and ties in 11, so low-entropy retention is not a dependable alternative either.
\item A byte-level cache (one entry per byte) loses much more than a patch cache at equal entry counts: at 32 entries recency costs $+0.2286$ \bpb{} against $+0.0133$ with patches. Patches win per byte of cache; bytes win only with about 4.5 times more cache.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{More training makes the penalty larger}\label{sec:scale}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering\includegraphics[width=\columnwidth]{figures/fig_scale.pdf}
\caption{Ten times more training (3,000 to 30,000 steps, WikiText with code mix) makes the entropy penalty about ten times larger (left) and the SnapKV-style gain several times larger (right). Bars are 95\% bootstrap intervals; three seeds of entropy patching, one of fixed stride.}\label{fig:scale}
\end{figure}
A natural objection is that a small, short-trained model reads little from its cache. We trained WikiText code-mix models for 30,000 steps. They score 1.2949, 1.2983 and 1.3118 \bpb{} and keeping high entropy costs $+0.0287$, $+0.0284$ and $+0.0238$ against random at 64 patches (against $+0.0020$ at 3,000 steps), with every interval excluding zero (Figure~\ref{fig:scale}). Any eviction also costs more (recency at 64 patches costs 0.018 \bpb{} over the full cache, from 0.002), and the fixed-stride model shows the same sign. The same experiment on TinyStories overfits (training loss 0.14 nats, validation \bpb{} 1.6493 against 0.7980) and we draw no conclusion from it. So a small model is not what made the entropy rule look bad. A longer context does not rescue it either: our first 16,384-byte models were under-trained and suggested the penalty fades, a conclusion withdrawn after tuning (Section~\ref{sec:challenges}).

\subsection{What works: attention, quantization, bounded memory}
\textbf{Attention-based scoring.}\label{sec:attn} Online, the SnapKV-style rule is better than recency in all 24 comparisons on the 12 entropy-patched models (by 0.0002 to 0.0077 \bpb, for example $-0.0013$ [$-0.0018$, $-0.0009$] on WikiText code mix, seed 0, 64 patches), and never worse than its offline approximation. With offline scoring it is also better than recency in 33 of 36 test-split cells (the other three tie) and, for the 30,000-step models, by 0.0051, 0.0057 and 0.0049 at 64 patches. Online H2O is worse than recency in 22 of 24: accumulated attention favours old patches that were popular early. The effect is small (for seed 0 on TinyStories at 32 patches recency costs 0.0098 over the full cache and SnapKV-style recovers 0.0041), but it is the one signal that beat recency, and it is attention, not surprise.

\textbf{Quantization and combination.} Uniform 4-bit K and V costs 0.0003 to 0.0014 \bpb{} over fp16 across ten models at 27\% of the bytes (uniform 3-bit 0.0014 to 0.0077, 2-bit 0.022 to 0.111). Entropy-guided precision tiers lose to random and to recency tiers in all ten models. Eviction and 4-bit together, mean of three seeds, are in Table~\ref{tab:combo}: a WikiText cache of 128 patches in 4-bit holds 7.4\% of the full cache bytes and costs $+0.0012$ \bpb{} with SnapKV-style eviction ($+0.0019$ with recency), while keeping high entropy costs $+0.0151$. Costs of eviction and quantization add roughly linearly. No setting that keeps high-entropy patches is on the bytes-against-\bpb{} frontier (Figure~\ref{fig:frontier}).

\textbf{Bounded-memory streaming.}\label{sec:stream} Three WikiText code-mix models trained on 4,096-byte chunks decoded 46 validation documents longer than 8,192 bytes with a fixed patch budget. Recency with 64 patches beats the chunked full-cache baseline in all three seeds (by 0.0030, 0.0032 and 0.0008 \bpb; with 256 patches by 0.0051, 0.0058 and 0.0035), because the reset loses the context at every chunk boundary and a small continuous window does not. SnapKV-style is better still at 64 patches (0.0014, 0.0008 and 0.0024 below recency). Random, H2O and keeping high entropy are worse than recency at every budget in all seeds (Figure~\ref{fig:stream}, left). On PG-19, with the 30,000-step model, 256 patches of recency beat the reset baseline by 0.0086 \bpb{} [0.0079, 0.0093] and the whole-book full cache by 0.145; the whole-book full cache is worse than the reset baseline (by 0.137), so the model does not use a context 24 times longer than it was trained on. Keeping high-entropy patches is $+0.093$ to $+0.122$ worse than recency. Keeping old patches means attending at distances never seen in training, and we did not renumber positions inside the cache as StreamingLLM does, so PG-19 cannot separate a poor entropy signal from poor long-distance attention.

\textbf{Memory and speed.} Figure~\ref{fig:memory} gives the measured savings. The fixed 786 KB byte-window caches and the entropy model (about 2.1 MB of window cache and 3.3M parameters) are not reduced, so at 64 patches they exceed the global cache. At full precision the patch cache is about 0.9 times the size of a BPE Transformer cache of the same width, and a byte-level Transformer needs about four times more.

\textbf{Compute per byte.}\label{sec:flops} Table~\ref{tab:flops} is an analytic count, not a measurement. The BLT is cheaper than a byte-level Transformer (11.1 against 30.9 MFLOPs per byte at a context of 1,024 bytes), but it is \emph{more} expensive than the BPE Transformer (7.4), because its local encoder and decoder run on every byte, and it needs the separate entropy model (8.5 MFLOPs per byte) when patches come from entropy; with it the total is 19.6. A 64-patch cache trims only the global attention term (0.3 MFLOPs at 1,024 bytes, 1.7 at 4,096). So the case for the BLT here is cache memory, not FLOPs.

\section{Challenges and how we handled them}\label{sec:challenges}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Optimistic baseline scoring.} The Part 2 \bpb{} counted the first token of each chunk, which is context only, making it 0.4 to 0.5\% too low. We kept the reported numbers for traceability, disclosed the correction (0.6978, 1.5257) and score every byte in Part 3.
\item \textbf{Seed variance as large as the effects.} A first single-seed result said entropy patching is 3.3\% worse than fixed stride on TinyStories; seed spread is 0.056 to 0.135 \bpb. We withdrew it, ran three seeds of every scheme and a stride-5 control, and paired every comparison by document.
\item \textbf{Cache accounting bug.} The first policy evaluation counted full-cache entries twice, so byte shares were half the true shares. It surfaced when eviction and quantization rows were put in one table and the full-cache sizes disagreed (1,669 KB against 839 KB). Fixed, evaluations rerun, all tables regenerated; \bpb{} was unaffected.
\item \textbf{A wrong conclusion from under-trained models.} The first 16,384-byte models scored 1.89 \bpb{}, worse than the 4k models, and suggested the entropy penalty fades with context. Tuning learning rate and batch size on validation gave 1.5843 and showed the penalty does not fade; the claim is withdrawn. The tuned models also saw four times more bytes, so context length and training amount are not separated.
\item \textbf{Overfitting at ten times the training.} 30,000 steps on TinyStories are 37 epochs. We kept the WikiText scale-up as the evidence and listed the TinyStories rows only for completeness.
\item \textbf{Code is gated and prose models cannot read it.} Models trained on prose score 7 to 8 \bpb{} on code. A login by a team member built the code set; we added a 15\% code mix to training, which costs about 0.05 \bpb{} on WikiText and 0.09 on TinyStories and leaves the eviction conclusion unchanged.
\item \textbf{A silent failure.} A CUDA out-of-memory error left a partly written entropy cache on disk while the queue carried on; reading the log caught it before training used zeros. The next queue now stops if the cache script fails.
\item \textbf{Offline attention policies.} Mask simulation cannot see the evicted cache, so the first H2O and SnapKV-style scores were approximations. We built the online \texttt{forward\_online} and tested it; the ranking held.
\item \textbf{Models that do not retrieve.} The needle test is uninformative; we report retention rates and conclude nothing about retrieval.
\item \textbf{PG-19 loading.} The standard dataset ships as a loading script that current libraries no longer run; we used a community mirror of the same test split and recorded the substitution.
\item \textbf{Cluster limits.} One job at a time with a 2-day limit. Runs checkpoint, handle a signal before the time limit, requeue and resume; the path was verified end to end under one job id (signal at step 1,864, resume, finish at step 24,000). Three earlier attempts to show it failed for avoidable reasons that the docs record.
\item \textbf{Reporting a negative result.} We tested the escape routes (other scores, own entropy, sink size, byte-level cache, more training, longer context, a protected recency window) before concluding, and added the attention rule that does beat recency.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item Models are small (13.3M parameters, mostly 3,000 steps). The 30,000-step models show the same sign with larger effects, but a much larger model, or a task that needs retrieval, could behave differently. The needle test shows our models do not retrieve.
\item TinyStories has no usable long-training result and no test split.
\item Cache sizes are computed from entry counts; only recency eviction was measured on a GPU, and quantization is simulated by rounding.
\item The 30,000-step fixed-stride model and the 16k models have one seed; the streaming runs use three seeds of one setting; PG-19 uses 20 books and out-of-domain models, and H2O and SnapKV-style were not run there because the step-decoding path has no attention policies.
\item Seed 0 of the prose-only models was trained on a different GPU from the other seeds.
\item The attention account of why entropy fails is a correlation; no intervention tests it, and it does not hold in every layer of every model.
\item A model that fits TinyStories memorises it, and no result from it should be read as general.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
What is new: a test of entropy-ranked cache retention on a byte-level patch model, against random and recency at equal bytes, with seeds, paired intervals and a test split; an online, tested attention-policy implementation on patch caches; and measured evidence that a fixed recency or SnapKV-style cache can stream documents several times longer than the training context better than resetting it. What failed: the central claim. The entropy that places patch boundaries is not a good signal for which patches to keep, and more training makes it worse. What is practical: 4-bit K and V plus a SnapKV-style or recency window keeps 7.4\% of the WikiText cache bytes for $+0.0012$ to $+0.0019$ \bpb. What we cannot say: whether the result holds for models orders of magnitude larger, or for tasks that need retrieval, because our models do not retrieve.
