\begin{abstract}
Byte-level language models eliminate the tokenizer and thereby avoid its characteristic failures on rare words, orthographic noise, source code and non-Latin scripts. They incur a corresponding cost in sequence length: a text is approximately four times longer in bytes than in byte-pair-encoding (BPE) tokens, and the key-value (KV) cache maintained during autoregressive decoding grows in proportion. We construct a 13.3M-parameter model of the Byte Latent Transformer (BLT) type, which retains one cache entry per patch of bytes rather than per byte, and examine the extent to which its cache can be compressed. The evaluation protocol comprises three random seeds per setting, paired bootstrap confidence intervals, random and recency baselines, a held-out test split and an analytic account of computation. Four results are positive. (i) Eviction combined with 4-bit quantization retains 7.4\% of a WikiText cache at a cost of $+0.0012$ bits per byte (\bpb). (ii) An online SnapKV-style policy, which ranks patches by the model's own attention, outperforms recency in 24 of 24 comparisons. (iii) A fixed-size cache sustains decoding of documents four times longer than the training context, and of 20 PG-19 books, with lower loss than context resetting, constant measured memory (0.59 MB against 136.5 MB) and constant time per byte. (iv) The BLT requires 2.8 times fewer floating-point operations per byte than a byte-level Transformer of equal width. We also evaluate a natural hypothesis: that the patches of highest next-byte entropy, the quantity the BLT already computes to place patch boundaries, are the most valuable to retain. The hypothesis is refuted. Entropy-ranked retention is worse than random selection in 68 of 72 comparisons and worse than recency in 70, and the deficit increases approximately tenfold under tenfold longer training. An error analysis attributes this to the selection of patches of only one or two bytes at the expense of those containing complete words. We state what is novel, what did not succeed, and what the present scale of experiment supports.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\textit{Task.} We study language modelling, that is, the prediction of each symbol of a text from the symbols that precede it. Models are evaluated in bits per byte (\bpb) on held-out documents, lower values being better. Production systems generally operate on subword tokens produced by BPE \citep{sennrich2016bpe}. Byte-level models dispense with the tokenizer and are consequently robust to rare words, typographical errors, code and non-English scripts \citep{xue2022byt5,yu2023megabyte,slagle2024spacebyte,pagnoni2024blt}. The cost is sequence length: a byte sequence is approximately four times longer than its BPE counterpart (4.0 bytes per token in our data).

\textit{Motivation.} During generation, a decoder stores the keys and values of all preceding positions in every layer. This KV cache, rather than the model weights, constrains batch size and context length at deployment. A byte-level Transformer of our width requires 151 MB per sequence at 16,384 bytes, compared with 38 MB for BPE. The BLT \citep{pagnoni2024blt} applies its large global Transformer to patches of bytes and therefore retains one cache entry per patch. To determine patch boundaries it already computes the entropy of the next-byte distribution, a measure of the model's predictive uncertainty.

\textit{Choice of problem.} The Part 1 proposal rested on three observations: the KV cache, rather than the weights, limits the number of users that can share a GPU and the context length that can be served; tokenizer-free models quadruple that cache; and the entropy used by the BLT to place patches is discarded after patching, whereas existing eviction methods rely on recency or on run-time attention scores. The problem is practically relevant, small enough to study from scratch on a single GPU, and informative irrespective of the outcome.

\textit{Research question.} Can this entropy additionally determine which patches to retain when the cache is full, or which to store at reduced precision? The signal is available at no extra cost, so a positive answer would yield a free compression criterion. Prior work uses uncertainty to set cache budgets or as one component of a composite score, in subword models (Section~\ref{sec:related}); to our knowledge, entropy-ranked retention has not been evaluated in byte-level models. We compare it with random, recency and attention-based criteria at equal cache size in bytes. It performs worse than all three, whereas the same experimental protocol yields four positive results (Table~\ref{tab:gain}).

\textit{Summary of findings.} Consider a passage of 1,000 bytes. A byte-level Transformer stores 1,000 cache entries; the present model groups the bytes into approximately 220 patches and stores 220 entries, about 2 MB. Retaining the patches of highest entropy is a poor criterion: it selects fragments of one or two bytes, discards whole words, and underperforms random selection. Two alternatives succeed. Ranking patches by the model's own attention and storing the retained entries in 4 bits reduces the cache to 7.4\% of its original size at a cost of $0.0012$ \bpb. A fixed-size cache permits decoding of documents four times longer than the training context, in constant memory, with lower loss than resetting the context at each boundary.

\section{Literature survey}\label{sec:related}
\textit{Tokens and bytes.} BPE merges frequent byte pairs into a fixed vocabulary \citep{sennrich2016bpe}. Token-free models operate on bytes: ByT5 \citep{xue2022byt5} is competitive with subword models and more robust to noise; MegaByte \citep{yu2023megabyte} combines a local model within patches with a global model between them; SpaceByte \citep{slagle2024spacebyte} inserts larger blocks after word-boundary bytes. The Byte Latent Transformer \citep{pagnoni2024blt} forms patches dynamically from the next-byte entropy of a small byte model, applies a large latent Transformer to them, and shows in a FLOP-controlled study up to 8B parameters that patches scale better than tokens. Our model is a simplified BLT with mean pooling in place of cross-attention, no n-gram hash embeddings, and 13.3M parameters.

\textit{KV cache eviction.} StreamingLLM retains a few initial ``sink'' positions and a recent window, which allows a model to stream far beyond its training length \citep{xiao2024streaming}. H2O retains the ``heavy hitters'', the positions with the largest accumulated attention \citep{zhang2023h2o}. SnapKV selects the positions attended to by the last queries of the prompt \citep{li2024snapkv}. All three operate on subword models. We implement recency with a sink, H2O and a SnapKV-style rule (attention from the last 16 patches) as policies over patch entries.

\textit{KV quantization.} KIVI quantizes keys per channel and values per token to 2 bits without tuning \citep{liu2024kivi}; KVQuant adds non-uniform and outlier-aware schemes for very long contexts \citep{hooper2024kvquant}. We use a simpler per-vector absmax quantizer and ask only whether entropy can allocate bit widths.

\textit{Uncertainty in cache management.} CONF-KV sets the per-step cache budget from the next-token entropy and ranks tokens by attention and recency, with random eviction as a control \citep{li2026confkv}. InfoKV combines predictive entropy and representation change with attention \citep{kai2026infokv}; counter-causal surprise scores cached tokens by how well later tokens predict them \citep{gould2026counter}; a learned controller assigns 2 to 16 bits per token from features that include entropy \citep{boroujeni2026bits}; and Scratchpad Patching adds temporary slots within byte patches according to next-byte uncertainty \citep{zheng2026scratchpad}. We read the first two works in full and the abstracts of the others, so our statement of difference from the latter is provisional. In all of these works entropy is one ingredient or sets a budget; to our knowledge none ranks the patches of a byte-level model by entropy alone against random and recency at equal size.

\textit{Relation to widely used models.} Widely used models read BPE tokens and address cache growth in the architecture: Llama 3, for example, uses grouped-query attention with 8 key-value heads, a fourfold reduction relative to one KV head per attention head in the 8B model \citep{llama3team2024}. Eviction and quantization are applied on top of such designs. We study the same bottleneck at a scale that one GPU can train from scratch (13.3M parameters, about 600 times smaller than an 8B model) and for raw bytes, which need about four times as many positions. We make no quality comparison with chat models; what transfers is the measurement protocol, the policies, and the finding that attention-guided rules are effective whereas entropy is not.


\textit{The classical baseline.} Interpolated Kneser-Ney smoothing \citep{kneser1995improved,chen1999smoothing} is the most accomplished classical n-gram method and furnishes a floor that any neural model ought to clear.

\textit{Sources and suitability.} TinyStories \citep{eldan2023tinystories} is a synthetic corpus of short stories generated by GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 (about 890 bytes each), which permits rapid training of small models. WikiText-103 \citep{merity2016pointer} contains long Wikipedia articles (about 18 KB each), suitable for experiments on long-context caches. The two lie at opposite ends of difficulty (our baselines reach 0.69 and 1.52 \bpb). The Stack \citep{kocetkov2022stack} (the Smol sample) adds code, and PG-19 \citep{rae2019compressive} supplies very long documents. The first three corpora serve for training and validation; the Wikipedia test split was used once, at the end.

\textit{Limitations and bias.} TinyStories is synthetic, narrow and repetitive, and long training overfits it (37 epochs of a 26.7 MB file). WikiText-103 is English and encyclopedic, and under-represents conversational and non-English text. The code sample consists of permissively licensed files from which only exact duplicates were removed, so near-duplicates may make validation code slightly easier. PG-19 is literary English of the early twentieth century and earlier, far from the WikiText distribution, and its \bpb{} is correspondingly high. The copy of WikiText training data used for the demonstration is limited to 3,000 articles to keep the download small; all reported runs use the full corpus.

\textit{Preprocessing.} WikiText articles were reassembled from the raw line export, which lacks article boundaries, and placeholder tokens such as \texttt{@-@} were restored. All text is NFC-normalised and exact duplicates are removed. Each split is stored as a single uint8 file of UTF-8 bytes with an offset array; documents are separated by a BOS byte and an attention mask; code is split by content hash so that duplicates never occur in more than one split. All thresholds and hyperparameters were tuned on validation data alone.

\textit{Model.} The network is of the BLT type and has 13,333,632 parameters. A local encoder (1 Transformer layer, width 256, 4 heads, rotary positions, a causal window of 256 bytes) reads the bytes. The encoder states of the bytes within a patch are mean-pooled and projected to width 384. A global Transformer (6 layers, width 384, 6 heads) operates causally over the patches. A local decoder (2 layers, width 256, window 256) predicts each byte from its own encoder state and the global output of the preceding patches. The global keys and values are the cache that we compress: one entry per patch, $2\cdot 6\cdot 384\cdot 2 = 9{,}216$ bytes at fp16. Step-by-step decoding equals the full forward pass to $10^{-5}$ (tested).

\textit{Patching.} Entropy patching follows the BLT: a separate byte model of 3.3M parameters (4 layers, width 256, window 512) supplies the next-byte entropy $H_i$, and a patch begins where $H_i$ exceeds a threshold tuned on validation data to give 4.5 bytes per patch (1.09 nats on TinyStories, 2.17 on WikiText). The decision depends only on earlier bytes (tested). Fixed-stride patching (4 or 5 bytes) serves as the control. Under entropy patching 67.8\% (TinyStories) and 66.7\% (WikiText) of patch starts fall on word starts, against 19.9\% and 16.3\% expected by chance.

\textit{Cache policies.} A policy retains at most $B$ patch entries. The first patch (the sink) and the 8 newest are always retained, and every policy selects among the remainder, so that differences between policies arise from the scoring signal and not from the window. The scores of old patches are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{recency}: newest first (as in StreamingLLM);
\item \emph{random}: three draws, averaged;
\item \emph{keep high entropy} (the hypothesis tested): the mean entropy of the patch, $s_j = |P_j|^{-1}\sum_{i\in P_j} H_i$, the largest values being retained;
\item \emph{keep low entropy}: the opposite control;
\item \emph{H2O}: attention received so far, summed over queries, heads and layers;
\item \emph{SnapKV-style}: attention received from the last 16 patches alone.
\end{itemize}
Entropy and recency policies are simulated by masks in a single forward pass and are tested to equal step-by-step decoding in which entries are actually removed. H2O and SnapKV-style policies require the evicted cache, so an online forward pass (\texttt{forward\_online}) runs the global layers patch by patch, selects the entry to discard before the new patch is attended to, and accumulates attention as it is produced (tested for causality and for equality with the full cache at a large budget).

\textit{Quantization.} Each K and V vector is stored with symmetric absmax scaling at $b$ bits plus one fp16 scale per head. The resulting sizes per entry are 9,216 bytes (fp16), 2,448 (4-bit, 27\%) and 1,296 (2-bit, 14\%). Tier policies assign a high width (4 bits) to a fraction of the patches and a low width (2 bits) to the rest, selected by recency, at random, or by entropy, and are compared with uniform widths. Eviction and quantization are combined by quantizing every entry that is retained.

\textit{Implementation and environment.} Models, policies, quantizer and training loop are our own PyTorch code, with rotary positions \citep{su2021roformer}, LayerNorm \citep{ba2016layernorm}, GELU \citep{hendrycks2016gelu}, a 4$\times$ MLP and pre-norm blocks \citep{vaswani2017attention}; training employs AdamW \citep{loshchilov2019adamw} and PyTorch \citep{paszke2019pytorch}. No pretrained checkpoint is used; every weight is trained from nothing. Hyperparameters (BLT): AdamW, learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay 0.01, 200 warm-up steps then cosine decay to 10\%, gradient clip 1.0, bf16 autocast, batch 8 of 4,096 bytes, 3,000 steps (30,000 for the long runs), seeds 0 to 2. Entropy model: learning rate $2\cdot10^{-3}$, batch 32 of 1,024 bytes, 3,000 steps, clip 1.0. Seed 0 of the prose-only models was trained on a laptop NVIDIA RTX 4060 (8 GB, Python 3.13, PyTorch 2.9) and the remaining runs on a cluster NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 (95 GB); the demonstrations and the analyses of Section~\ref{sec:errors} run on CPU.

\textit{Streaming.} For long documents the fixed-size cache processes 16,384-byte chunks (and, for PG-19, 100,000 bytes one byte at a time with the incremental decoding routine), and is compared with a baseline that resets the context every 4,096 bytes, the training length.

\section{Experiments}\label{sec:exp}
\subsection{Setup, metrics and baselines}
\textit{Training budgets.} The principal models are trained on about 98M bytes (3,000 steps). Some WikiText code-mix models are trained for 30,000 steps (about 983M bytes, 1.9 epochs) and at a context of 16,384 bytes. The same checkpoint yields identical scores on the laptop and the cluster GPU.

\textit{Metric.} Bits per byte is the total negative base-2 log-likelihood of a document divided by its length in bytes. It is independent of the tokenizer, so the 4-gram, the BPE Transformer and the byte models are compared on identical text, and it equals the code length per byte that an arithmetic coder driven by the model would achieve \citep{deletang2023compression}. Its limitations are as follows. It measures prediction and not downstream use such as retrieval. Differences between cache policies are small (at most 0.03 \bpb), so every comparison is paired by document and reported with a 95\% bootstrap interval over documents (2,000 resamples). The Part 2 BPE figure is optimistic by 0.4 to 0.5\%, because the first token of each 256-token chunk serves only as context while its bytes are counted (corrected values 0.6978 and 1.5257). Cache size is the mean resident global KV size computed from entry counts and bit widths, and was measured on a GPU for recency alone.

\textit{Baselines.} The classical baseline is an interpolated Kneser-Ney byte 4-gram; the modern baseline is a 13.8M-parameter BPE Transformer (6 layers, 6 heads, width 384, 8k vocabulary, 256-token context, 3,000 steps). The cache baselines are the full cache, recency with a sink, random selection, H2O, a SnapKV-style rule, and uniform 8, 4, 3 and 2 bits. The Transformer was trained before gradient clipping was introduced (clipping gives 0.6826 on TinyStories), so the modern baseline is slightly handicapped.

\subsection{Baselines and base models}\label{sec:base}
Table~\ref{tab:base} gives the comparison. All neural models are far ahead of the 4-gram (a gap of 1.0 to 1.1 \bpb{} on TinyStories and 0.65 to 0.9 on WikiText). At equal training the BPE Transformer outperforms our BLT-style model on both sets (0.69 against 0.80 on TinyStories, 1.52 against 1.68 on WikiText); our model is smaller, trained more briefly, and lacks the hashed n-gram embeddings and cross-attention of the original BLT, so we make no claim of superiority over BPE at equal training. With tenfold training (a comparison that favours the BLT, since the Transformer retains 3,000 steps) the WikiText code-mix BLT reaches 1.29 to 1.31, and its cache is the one we compress below.

Entropy patching and fixed-stride patching, three seeds each (mean full-cache \bpb): on WikiText 1.6958 (entropy) against 1.7347 (stride 4) and 1.7629 (stride 5), entropy patching being lower in all three same-seed pairs and using fewer cache entries per byte than stride 4; on TinyStories 0.8183 against 0.8237 and 0.8521. Seed ranges are 0.056 and 0.028 for entropy patching and 0.135 and 0.044 for stride 4, so the WikiText gap of 0.039 is comparable to a seed range. We therefore regard the advantage as likely but not established. On TinyStories, entropy patching merely ties stride 4. An initial single-seed comparison that placed entropy patching 3.3\% behind stride 4 on TinyStories did not survive the additional seeds and was withdrawn.

Table~\ref{tab:evict} and Figure~\ref{fig:score} show that entropy-ranked retention is outperformed by random selection and by recency at every budget. The result is robust across all the evidence gathered:
\begin{itemize}
\item Over 72 comparisons (9 models in each of 4 settings, 2 budgets), keeping high entropy is worse than recency with the interval above zero in 70 and indistinguishable in 2, and worse than random in 68 and indistinguishable in 4; it is never better than either. The paired difference against random is $+0.0003$ to $+0.0245$ \bpb.
\item It holds in every seed; for fixed-stride models (so it is not caused by the unevenness of entropy patches); with the code mix; on the WikiText test split (all 36 model and budget cells are worse than random and recency); at 16,384 bytes ($+0.016$ to $+0.021$ against random at 64 to 512 patches); and for a recency-protected variant (newest half retained, the other half chosen by entropy: worse than recency in 23 of 24 cells).
\item Ablations do not change the conclusion (three seeds): the model's own entropy, the first-byte score, the mean score and sink sizes 0 and 4 are worse than random in all seeds on both datasets; the maximum rule is worse in 3 of 3 (WikiText) and 2 of 3 (TinyStories) seeds; only the sum rule on TinyStories is ever better (one seed, $-0.0024$).
\item Keeping \emph{low} entropy is better than keeping high entropy in 42 of 72 comparisons, worse in 19 and tied in 11, so low-entropy retention is not a dependable alternative either.
\item A byte-level cache (one entry per byte) loses far more than a patch cache at equal entry counts: at 32 entries recency costs $+0.2286$ \bpb{} against $+0.0133$ with patches. Patches are better per byte of cache; the byte-level cache is competitive only with about 4.5 times as many bytes.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{More training makes the penalty larger}\label{sec:scale}
A possible objection is that a small, briefly trained model makes little use of its cache. WikiText code-mix models trained for 30,000 steps score 1.2949, 1.2983 and 1.3118 \bpb{}, and keeping high entropy costs $+0.0287$, $+0.0284$ and $+0.0238$ against random at 64 patches (compared with $+0.0020$ at 3,000 steps), every interval excluding zero. Any eviction also costs more (recency at 64 patches costs 0.018 \bpb{} over the full cache, up from 0.002), and the fixed-stride model shows the same sign. The TinyStories version overfits (training loss 0.14 nats, validation \bpb{} 1.6493) and is not used. A longer context does not change the conclusion either: our first 16,384-byte models were under-trained and suggested that the penalty diminishes, a conclusion withdrawn after tuning (Section~\ref{sec:challenges}).

\subsection{What works: attention, quantization, bounded memory}
\textit{Attention-based scoring.}\label{sec:attn} Online, the SnapKV-style rule outperforms recency in all 24 comparisons on the 12 entropy-patched models (by 0.0002 to 0.0077 \bpb, for example $-0.0013$ [$-0.0018$, $-0.0009$] on WikiText code mix, seed 0, 64 patches), and is never worse than its offline approximation. With offline scoring it also beats recency in 33 of 36 test-split cells (the other three are ties) and, for the 30,000-step models, by 0.0051, 0.0057 and 0.0049 at 64 patches.

Online H2O is worse than recency in 22 of 24 comparisons, since accumulated attention favours old patches that were popular early. The effect is small (for seed 0 on TinyStories at 32 patches, recency costs 0.0098 over the full cache and the SnapKV-style rule recovers 0.0041), but it is the only signal that outperformed recency, and it is attention rather than surprise.


\textit{Quantization and combination.} Uniform 4-bit K and V costs 0.0003 to 0.0014 \bpb{} over fp16 across ten models at 27\% of the bytes (uniform 3-bit 0.0014 to 0.0077, 2-bit 0.022 to 0.111). Entropy-guided precision tiers are worse than random and recency tiers in all ten models. Eviction combined with 4-bit storage (mean of three seeds) is shown in Table~\ref{tab:combo}: a WikiText cache of 128 patches in 4-bit holds 7.4\% of the full cache bytes and costs $+0.0012$ \bpb{} with SnapKV-style eviction ($+0.0019$ with recency), whereas keeping high entropy costs $+0.0151$. No setting that retains high-entropy patches lies on the bytes-against-\bpb{} frontier.

\textit{Bounded-memory streaming.}\label{sec:stream} Three WikiText code-mix models trained on 4,096-byte chunks decoded 46 validation documents longer than 8,192 bytes under a fixed patch budget. Recency with 64 patches outperforms the chunked full-cache baseline in all three seeds (by 0.0030, 0.0032 and 0.0008 \bpb; with 256 patches by 0.0051, 0.0058 and 0.0035), because the reset discards the context at every chunk boundary whereas a small continuous window does not. The SnapKV-style rule is better still at 64 patches (0.0014, 0.0008 and 0.0024 below recency). Random selection, H2O and keeping high entropy are worse than recency at every budget in all seeds (Figure~\ref{fig:stream}, left).

On PG-19, with the 30,000-step model, 256 patches of recency outperform the reset baseline by 0.0086 \bpb{} [0.0079, 0.0093] and the whole-book full cache by 0.145; the whole-book full cache is worse than the reset baseline (by 0.137), indicating that the model makes no use of a context 24 times longer than its training context. Keeping high-entropy patches is $+0.093$ to $+0.122$ worse than recency. Retaining old patches implies attending across distances not seen in training, and we did not renumber positions inside the cache as StreamingLLM does, so PG-19 cannot separate a poor entropy signal from poor long-distance attention.


\textit{Memory and speed.} A bounded cache holds the global cache at 0.59 to 2.36 MB (64 to 256 patches) while a full cache reaches 136.5 MB at 65,536 bytes, and time per byte stays at 0.9 ms against a rise to 2.0 ms (measured on a GPU, one stream, real eviction of recency). Some fixed costs are not reduced: the 786 KB byte-window caches and the entropy model (about 2.1 MB of window cache, 3.3M parameters), which at 64 patches exceed the global cache. At full precision the patch cache is about 0.9 times the cache of a BPE Transformer of the same width, and a byte-level Transformer requires about four times as much.

\textit{Compute per byte.}\label{sec:flops} The analytic account is a count of operations, not a measurement. The BLT requires fewer operations than a byte-level Transformer (11.1 against 30.9 MFLOPs per byte at a context of 1,024 bytes) but more than the BPE Transformer (7.4), because its local encoder and decoder run on every byte; when patches are produced by entropy it also requires the separate entropy model (8.5 MFLOPs per byte), giving a total of 19.6. A 64-patch cache reduces only the global attention term (0.3 MFLOPs at 1,024 bytes, 1.7 at 4,096). The advantage of the BLT here is therefore cache memory, not FLOPs.



\textit{Retrieval.} In a planted-name test (a random name repeated after 400 to 3,000 filler bytes) the models score the same with and without the earlier mention, even at 30,000 steps, so that no policy can be shown to help and nothing about retrieval may be concluded. The entropy rule does retain the name's patches more often than random (45\% against 31\% at 64 patches and 400 bytes), but the model profits nothing from them.

\section{Scope of the evidence}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textit{Scale and task.} The models have 13.3M parameters and mostly 3,000 training steps (30,000-step models: same sign, larger effects). They do not retrieve named facts (a planted-name test could not tell any policy from another), so claims concern prediction quality.
\item \textit{Data and measurement.} TinyStories has no test split; PG-19 rests on 20 books scored by models trained on other text; cache sizes are computed from entry counts (measured on a GPU for recency only); quantization is simulated; FLOPs are analytic.
\item \textit{Seeds.} The 30,000-step fixed-stride and 16,384-byte models have one seed apiece; streaming uses three seeds of one setting.
\item \textit{Mechanism.} The accounts of why entropy fails (attention, fragment length) are correlational; no intervention separates them.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
\textit{Novel.} A controlled evaluation of entropy-ranked cache retention on a byte-level patch model (multiple seeds, paired intervals, a test split); an online, tested implementation of attention policies over patch caches; and measured evidence that a fixed cache streams documents several times longer than the training context with lower loss than a context reset.

\textit{Improved.} Eviction with 4-bit quantization retains 7.4\% of the cache for $+0.0012$ to $+0.0019$ \bpb; SnapKV-style eviction outperforms recency in 24 of 24 comparisons; memory and time per byte remain constant under a bounded cache; and the BLT requires 2.8 times fewer FLOPs per byte than a byte-level Transformer.

\textit{Negative.} The entropy that places patch boundaries is not a useful criterion for selecting patches to retain, and longer training increases its penalty; it retains mainly fragments of one and two bytes. The model's own attention is the signal that does select patches usefully, and with it the cache of a byte-level model can be made small and kept bounded as the text grows (verified to 100,000 bytes).
